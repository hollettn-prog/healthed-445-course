A Complete Lesson

Begin with the student performance you want to see. Then plan the model and practice that prepare students for it. A lesson plan makes the connections visible: teacher action, learner action, and the evidence you will collect. Label planned decisions as plans; you do not need to teach this lesson to complete the module.

What to adapt

The product activity already asks students to examine claims, advertising, and a recommendation. For our lesson, focus its questions on the supplied planner-related claims and source details. Students compare the sources and justify a judgment; they do not need to select or purchase a real product.

Keep or revise questions that help students examine the claim, creator, evidence, and purpose. The original activity refers to advertising techniques discussed in the textbook. That chapter is not required. Revise or remove that question, and questions about purchase restrictions, when they do not serve this goal or the needed information is not supplied. Treat missing details as things to verify rather than inventing them.

Ms. Rivera’s Class

Use this fictional class of 26 eighth graders. After viewing two wellness posts, 18 trusted the post with more followers, six checked its creator, and nine noticed its date. The counts overlap. Students have different reading confidence, and some have limited home internet. One class lasts 45 minutes; printed posts and source details are available.

Working goal: Given fictional health posts and source details, students will compare trustworthiness and justify a judgment using at least two relevant considerations. They will explain a limitation or detail that still needs checking. Consult selected source-evaluation work in NHES Standard 3; this lesson does not assess the full standard.

Source Cards

These fictional cards provide the source information for your adaptation. Use A and B for modeling and guided comparison. Keep C for a new individual comparison with B; do not show a worked answer to that comparison first.

A: ResetTimer Planner

A company selling planners posts: “Our planner reduces school stress for every student.” The page names the company, shows a purchase link, and includes three customer testimonials. It provides no research report or comparison data for the universal claim.

B: School Planning Notes

A fictional school student-support team suggests listing assignments and choosing a first step. The page names its authors’ roles and describes the advice as a planning suggestion. It says students’ experiences vary and provides no study showing that a specific product reduces stress.

C: Student Newsletter

A student editor interviews two volunteers who say a planner helped them feel more organized. The article identifies them as volunteers, includes no comparison group, and does not claim the results apply to everyone. No sales link appears.

Worked Planning Decision

A learner writes, “A is reliable because it has testimonials.” A useful model connects a detail to a judgment: testimonials describe selected customers’ experiences; they do not establish that the planner reduces stress for every student. Invite the learner to revise the reason rather than copy a finished conclusion.

During partner work, have each learner explain one source detail and why it matters. Then collect an individual comparison. Reading the cards aloud or accepting an oral explanation can preserve the evaluation goal; supplying the judgment would replace the reasoning you need to observe.

A Possible 45-Minute Sequence

Illustrative timing, not a required script
Phase Minutes Student work
Opening and goal 5 Read the task and rehearse the partner routine.
Model 6 Follow a model of one A/B comparison decision and identify a detail-to-judgment connection.
Guided comparison 12 Each partner explains a detail and a reason.
Feedback and revision 7 Revise a weak connection using feedback.
Individual application 10 Compare B/C and explain a judgment and limitation.
Review and close 5 Submit the individual response; identify the next question.

If time runs short, shorten a repeated example or report-out. Protect essential practice and a brief individual response. Record missing evidence and arrange a follow-up rather than assuming that the class met the goal.
